General Appearance

The physical examination map, level of consciousness and build.

Physical Examination→General Examination+Local Examination

General examination

  1. General appearance.
  2. Vital measurements.
  3. Anthropometric measurements.
  4. Head-to-toe examination.

Local examination

  1. Cardiac examination.
  2. Chest examination.
  3. Abdominal examination.
  4. Neurological examination.

First look

Assess conscious level, including gait, behavior and movements in a moving child. Note the child's build: average, underbuilt or overbuilt. Then observe facial expression, position in bed, color, odors and sounds.

Clinical assessment

In an older child, assess alertness and orientation to time, place and person. In a small infant, observe activity and play. An altered child may be delirious, confused, stuporous or comatose.

Pediatric Glasgow Coma Scale

Assess eye opening (4), verbal response (5) and motor response (6); maximum total 15.

A score of 8 or less indicates severe impairment and needs urgent critical care assessment.

02

Abnormal Facial Features

Facial clues seen during the first inspection.

  • A child may appear toxic, dehydrated, wasted or edematous.
  • Down syndrome: characteristic facial features.
  • Thalassemia: characteristic facial changes may be present.
  • Marasmus: a wizened appearance with loss of the buccal fat pad.
  • Congenital hypothyroidism: coarse facial features.
Look systematically: describe the facial findings you actually see rather than using a label alone.

Abnormal Positions in Bed

Recognize posture and the associated clinical context.

Orthopneic position

May occur in heart failure with pulmonary congestion and impaired gas exchange.

Opisthotonos

Neck hyperextension and arching of the back, with flexion of the lower limbs; described here with meningitis.

Squatting

Seen with tetralogy of Fallot and other cyanotic congenital heart diseases with low pulmonary blood flow.

Tripod position

Forward-leaning seated posture that recruits accessory respiratory muscles; described with bronchial asthma and acute epiglottitis.

Another positionPrayer position may be seen in pericardial effusion.

Abnormal Odors

Consider where the smell originates.

Odors may come from the breath, urine, stool, sputum, vomitus, skin or genital secretions.

Body odor

  • Acetone: ketones in diabetic ketoacidosis.
  • Fishy smell: toxic chemicals in chronic renal failure.
  • Kerosene: kerosene poisoning.

Mouth or breath

  • Halitosis: gingivitis, dental caries, chronic tonsillitis or suppurative lung disease.
  • Fetor hepaticus: described as rotten-apple breath in hepatic failure.

Urine

  • Acetone: may occur in diabetic ketoacidosis.
  • Bad odor: may accompany urinary tract infection.

Pallor

Where to look, how to grade it and why it occurs.

Pallor is unusual paleness of the skin and mucous membranes.

Best sites to inspect

  • Oral mucosa, tongue and inner lips (with gentle pressure).
  • Inner surface of the lower eyelids.
  • Palms and palmar creases, especially when fingers are stretched, and fingernails.

Clinical grading

  • Mild to moderate: pale mucosa, but pink palmar creases.
  • Severe: faint, pale palmar creases.

Possible causes

  • Reduced red blood cell number: anemic causes.
  • Reduced blood flow or oxygenation: shock, hypoxia, vagal stimulation, hypothermia or fear.
  • Increased skin thickness: edema or myxedema.
  • Skin pigmentation can affect the appearance of pallor.

Cyanosis

Central, peripheral and differential patterns.

Cyanosis is a bluish discoloration of skin and mucous membranes associated with reduced hemoglobin. The classic teaching threshold is about 5 g/dL of reduced hemoglobin.

Central cyanosis

Involves the mucosae and extremities. Consider impaired pulmonary function (for example, alveolar hypoventilation), cardiac right-to-left shunts (cyanotic congenital heart disease or pulmonary AV fistula), and hemoglobin abnormalities such as methemoglobinemia.

Peripheral cyanosis

Involves the extremities but spares the mucosae. Poor peripheral circulation increases oxygen extraction, as with cold exposure, congestive heart failure or arterial obstruction (Raynaud phenomenon or emboli).

Differential cyanosis

  1. Hands blue, feet pink: a pattern described with transposition of the great arteries and coarctation of the aorta.
  2. Hands pink, feet blue: may occur with a PDA and reversed shunt due to pulmonary hypertension.
Clinical distinction: check the mucosae as well as the hands and feet when describing the distribution.

Jaundice & Kramer Zones

Recognition, examination sites and the cephalocaudal pattern.

Jaundice is yellowish discoloration of skin and mucous membranes. The teaching figures in this handout are clinical visibility at roughly >2 mg/dL in older children and >5 mg/dL in neonates.

How to examine

Blanch the skin and inspect the sclerae, the palate and underside of the tongue, and the skin. In neonates, pay attention to the tip of the nose, palms and soles.

Kramer's index

In newborns, jaundice may spread from the face down the body. The zones below are an approximate visual teaching guide, not a bilirubin measurement.

01 · Face & neck~5 mg/dL
02 · Upper trunk6–10 mg/dL
03 · Lower trunk & thighs10–15 mg/dL
04 · Legs15–18 (<20) mg/dL
05 · Palms & soles≥20 mg/dL
Clinical note: visual extent is an estimate. Use an objective bilirubin measurement for clinical decisions.
