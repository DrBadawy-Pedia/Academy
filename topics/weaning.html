01

Definition & Timing

The meaning, timing and purpose of complementary feeding.

Definition

Weaning is the gradual introduction of complementary feeds while continuing breast feeding or formula feeding.

Readiness · around 5–6 months in sourceContinue milk feedingGradual introductionSource endpoint · 1.5–2 years
Aims of weaning
  1. Meet growth needs for foods, minerals and vitamins that milk alone cannot supply after 4–6 months.
  2. Train the gastrointestinal tract to digest starch and other solid foods.
  3. Teach independence through spoon and cup use.
Does weaning mean suddenly stopping breast or formula feeding?
No. The source defines it as gradual introduction of complementary feeds while milk feeding continues.
02

Signs of Readiness

Developmental abilities that support safe complementary feeding.

01

Postural control

  • No signs of hypotonia.
  • Sits alone or with minimal support.
  • Good head control.
02

Oral readiness

  • Loss of the tongue-thrust reflex.
03

Interest in food

  • Indicates a desire for food by leaning forward and opening the mouth.
Source timing

The source states that developmental readiness is usually achieved at 5–6 months and lists age around 5 or 6 months.

03

How to Carry Out Weaning

Gradual progression, suitable texture and family planning.

01

Timing

Postpone weaning in hot weather and during convalescence from acute illness, especially gastroenteritis.

02

Progression

Proceed gradually rather than introducing multiple major changes at once.

03

Texture

Added food should be mashed or semisolid, strained or puréed.

04

Presentation

Present food attractively, for example with colored spoons and plates.

05

Family context

Plan and time food additions according to the family’s abilities.

06

Nutrient density

Foods should be rich in protein, essential fatty acids, vitamins—especially vitamin D—and minerals, especially iron and zinc.

04

Starting Complementary Foods

The source sequence, portions and introduction of new foods.

Source’s suggested first food

Iron-fortified rice cereal: described as well tolerated, gluten-free, low in protein and the grain least likely to cause an allergic reaction.

01

Start small

Begin with one teaspoon of iron-fortified rice cereal at one feeding.

02

Increase gradually

Increase to 3–4 tablespoons once or twice daily. The source also gives a general start of 1 tsp/day, gradually increasing to 1–2 tsp/day.

03

One at a time

Introduce puréed fruits and vegetables one at a time, 3–5 days apart, and observe for allergy or sensitivity.

Do not

Do not add sugar or honey to cereal. Do not put cereal in a bottle or infant feeder.

05

Introducing Allergenic Foods

The sequence and foods highlighted by the source.

When?

After the infant tolerates a few less-allergenic complementary foods such as rice cereal and puréed fruits or vegetables.

YogurtHen’s eggsSoyWheatPeanutFish
Source statement

Highly allergenic foods can be introduced gradually and carefully to asymptomatic high-risk infants without testing.

06

Foods to Avoid in the First Year

High-yield list from the source.

High salt / sugar

  • Canned foods.
  • Juices.

Specific hazards

  • Honey: botulism.
  • Spinach: nitrites.

Milk drink

  • Fresh cow’s milk.
Why is honey avoided in the first year?
Risk of infant botulism.
Which milk is avoided as a drink in the first year?
Fresh cow’s milk.
