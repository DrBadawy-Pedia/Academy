Foundations

Definitions, learning objectives and the stages of growth.

Growth

Increase in the size of the organism due to an increase in the size and/or number of its constituent cells.

Development

Maturation of functions, acquisition of skills and adaptation to stress.

Learning objectives
  1. Evaluate the normal growth pattern.
  2. Understand growth curves and their uses.
  3. Recognize developmental milestones used for follow-up.
Stages of growth and development
First 8 weeks

Embryonic period

Devoted primarily to organogenesis.

Teratogenic exposure may cause abortion or congenital anomalies.
9-28 weeks

Early fetal period

Rapid growth mainly in length.

Teratogenic exposure may cause fetal death, stillbirth, prematurity or IUGR.
28-40 weeks

Late fetal period

Rapid growth mainly in weight.

Fetal well-being and maternal health remain major determinants.

Factors Affecting Growth

A practical prenatal-to-postnatal framework.

Before delivery

  • Familial factors.
  • Racial factors.

During pregnancy

  • Maternal disease: TORCH, diabetes and hypertension.
  • Maternal drugs, irradiation or malnutrition.
  • Maternal hypoxia.

After delivery

  • Age and sex.
  • Hormonal influences.
  • Nutritional and psychological status.
  • Chronic illness and socioeconomic factors.
Hormonal pattern

Fetal growth is influenced by chorionic gonadotropins and placental growth factors; infancy is influenced by growth hormone and thyroxine; adolescence is accelerated by sex hormones.

Weight Assessment

Normal pattern, major milestones and expected-weight formulas.

  • Average full-term newborn weight is 3 kg, with a normal range of 2.501-4 kg.
  • About 7-10% of birth weight is lost during the first 4 days because of fluid and meconium loss and initially limited feeding.
  • Birth weight is usually regained by day 10, within a normal range of 7-14 days.

When should birth weight be regained?
Usually by the 10th day of life, with a normal range of 7-14 days.
When does birth weight double, triple and quadruple?
It doubles at 4 months, triples at 1 year and quadruples at 2 years.
04

Length & Body Proportions

Linear growth, nutritional interpretation and proportionality.

Normal linear growth

  • Standing height is measured using a stadiometer.
  • Length is about 50 cm at birth, 75 cm at 1 year and 90 cm at 2 years.
  • From 2 years until adolescence, growth is approximately 4-5 cm/year.
  • Expected height from 2-12 years: (age × 6) + 77 cm.
  • By 13 years, birth length is approximately tripled to about 150 cm.

Weight-length interpretation

  • Low weight with normal length suggests recent nutritional deficiency.
  • Normal weight with low length suggests old nutritional deficiency.
  • Low weight with low length suggests old and recent nutritional deficiency.
  • Recumbent length is about 0.5 cm more than standing height due to joint compression.
Upper segment / Lower segment

Lower segment is measured from the pubic symphysis to the heel; upper segment is calculated by subtracting lower segment from stature.

Abnormal US/LS ratio

  • High ratio (short limbs): achondroplasia or rickets.
  • Low ratio (short trunk): mucopolysaccharidoses or hypogonadism.
  • Normal ratio: proportionate dwarfism such as hypopituitarism or constitutional dwarfism.

Arm span / Height

  • Children: arm span is 1-2 cm shorter than height.
  • Adolescents: arm span equals height.
  • Adults: arm span exceeds height by about 5 cm.
  • Useful for distinguishing proportionate from disproportionate short stature.

Bone Age Assessment

Ossification centers and clinical interpretation.

Ossification timeline

  • Fetal bone ossification begins during the 5th intrauterine month.
  • Calcaneus: 5 months; talus: 7 months; cuboid: 8 months.
  • Proximal tibia and lower femur: 9 months.
  • Capitate and hamate appear during the first year.
  • Number of carpal ossification centers ≈ age in years + 1 until age 6.
  • The eighth and final center appears between 10-12 years.

Clinical value

Bone age provides a maturational reference that helps distinguish delayed from advanced skeletal development when assessing abnormal growth.

Exam pearl

Interpret bone age alongside height, growth velocity, pubertal status and the child’s clinical context.

Delayed bone age

  • Growth hormone deficiency.
  • Hypothyroidism.
  • Malnutrition.
  • Chronic illness.
  • Constitutional delay.

Advanced bone age

  • Growth hormone excess.
  • Hyperthyroidism.
  • Simple obesity.

Growth Charts

Centiles, growth velocity and clinical uses.

Definition

A growth chart arranges a particular child’s measurement in comparison with children of the same age and sex.

Conceptual centile curves3rd · 50th · 97th centiles

Interpretation

The 50th centile represents the common central value for age. Values below the 3rd centile or above the 97th centile are considered abnormal and require clinical interpretation.

Growth velocity

The rate of growth per unit of time for the same child. It requires at least two measurements separated by a known interval, such as 3, 6 or 12 months.

Examples

Weight-for-age, length-for-age and weight-for-length charts. Body measurements and developmental characteristics may each have their own specific curves.

Value of growth charts
  1. Detect children who require investigation.
  2. Provide an easy method for growth assessment.
  3. Detect protein-energy malnutrition early through a flat curve and monitor response to therapy.
  4. Support research.
What centile values are considered abnormal?
Values below the 3rd centile or above the 97th centile.
What is required to calculate growth velocity?
At least two measurements in the same child with a known time interval between them.

Developmental Milestones

A practical age-by-age summary from birth to five years.

At birth
  • Turns head from side to side.
2 months
  • Smiles at the sound of your voice.
  • Follows you with the eyes as you move slowly around the room.
3 months
  • Raises head and chest when prone.
  • Grasps objects.
  • Smiles at other people.
4 months
  • Babbles, laughs and tries to imitate sounds.
  • Holds the head steady.
5–6 months
  • Rolls from prone to back, then from back to prone.
  • Moves objects from hand to hand.
6–7 months
  • Starts sitting.
  • Responds to own name.
  • Moves eyes to a rapidly moving object.
  • Finds partially hidden objects.
8–9 months
  • Sits without support.
  • Crawls.
  • Babbles “mama” and “dada”.
12 months
  • Walks with or without support.
  • Says at least one word.
  • Enjoys imitating people.
18 months
  • Walks independently.
  • Drinks from a cup.
  • Says at least 15 words.
  • Points to body parts.
2 years
  • Runs and jumps.
  • Speaks in two-word sentences.
  • Follows simple instructions.
  • Begins make-believe play.
3 years
  • Climbs well.
  • Speaks in multiword sentences.
  • Sorts objects by shape and color.
4 years
  • Gets along with people outside the family.
  • Draws circles and squares.
  • Rides a tricycle.
5 years
  • Tells name and address.
  • Jumps, hops and skips.
  • Gets dressed.
  • Counts 10 or more objects.
When should a child use two-word sentences?
At about 2 years.
What are key milestones at 12 months?
Walking with or without support, saying at least one word and enjoying imitation.
