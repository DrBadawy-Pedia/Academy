01

Definition & Indications

What artificial feeding means and when it may be used.

Definition

Artificial feeding is the practice of feeding an infant with infant formula rather than breast milk. The source stresses that breast feeding is superior to artificial feeding.

Indications

Contraindication to breast feeding

When a maternal or infant contraindication prevents breast feeding.

Complementary or supplementary feeding

Artificial milk may complete a breast feed or replace one or more breast feeds.

Other situations

  • Death or absence of the mother.
  • Multiple births.
  • Diseases, especially galactosemia.
How does the source define artificial feeding?
Feeding an infant with infant formula rather than breast milk.

03

Fresh Animal Milk

Cow’s milk processing, evaporated milk and goat’s milk.

Why avoid whole cow’s milk before 1 year?

Cow’s milk protein intolerance; increased iron deficiency anemia; high renal solute load; difficult digestion and unsuitable nutrient profile; microbial contamination; and increased frequency of diarrhea, rickets, respiratory infections and allergic disease.

01

Raw fresh cow’s milk

High in saturated fatty acids, sodium, phosphate and other ash, and low in long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids.

02

Pasteurized milk

Heating at 65°C for 30 minutes followed by rapid cooling. It destroys pathogenic bacteria but not spores, and modifies casein to form smaller, softer curds.

03

Homogenized milk

Milk is passed through fine holes under pressure to break large fat globules, preventing butter separation.

04

Evaporated & condensed milk

Water is removed until milk reaches one-third or one-half its original volume. It stores in the can without refrigeration, produces smaller/softer casein curds and makes lactalbumin less allergenic. The source states dilution 1 in 10 by volume, preparation one feed at a time and no boiling.

05

Goat’s milk

Advantages in source: more digestible, more essential fatty acids and described as hypoallergenic, though not a treatment for milk allergy.

Disadvantages: low folic acid causing megaloblastic anemia, brucellosis risk requiring boiling, and low caloric value.

04

Dried Powdered Milk

Advantages, preparation and the main formula categories.

Advantages of dried milk
  1. Sterile and of constant composition.
  2. Easy to store and handle; can be made fresh for each feed.
  3. Drying produces more digestible curds and makes lactalbumin less allergenic.
  4. Can remain in an open tin for some time without refrigeration.
  5. Fortified with vitamins and minerals; some contain LCPUFA, taurine, carnitine, nucleotides, prebiotics and probiotics.
Source preparation rule1 measure (4 g) + 1 oz (30 cc)Previously boiled water; described as a 1-in-8 dilution.
Full creamFat ≈ 4%Dried whole milk; not suitable below 1 year.
Half creamFat ≈ 2%Listed for the first 4 months and hot countries.

Skimmed milk

Fat <0.5%; unsuitable for normal infants. Listed for fat intolerance, premature/LBW infants, protein-calorie malnutrition, recurrent diarrhea and recovery from gastroenteritis.

Humanized milk

Modified toward human milk: softer protein curds, finely dispersed fat with vegetable oils and more LCPUFA, adjusted Ca/P ratio, added vitamin D and iron. Listed for normal infants, large premature infants (2–2.5 kg) and mild malnutrition.

What is the fat content of full-, half- and skimmed milk?
About 4%, about 2%, and less than 0.5%, respectively.
05

Special Types of Milk

Acidified, protein and purpose-specific formulas.

A

Acidified milk

Chemical: weak acid such as lactic acid. Biological: fermentation by Lactobacillus and Bifidum bacteria (probiotics).

  • Needs less HCl for digestion.
  • Strengthens the gastric acid barrier.
  • Produces small, digestible casein curds.
  • Enhances calcium and iron absorption.
  • May be full cream, half cream or skimmed.
  • Listed for recurrent diarrhea, recovery from GE, PCM, premature and LBW infants.
B

Protein milk

Example: Sustagen. Prepared by adding milk curd or dry casein to buttermilk. Rich in protein, low in fat and very low in carbohydrate; listed for debilitated infants and infants with diarrhea.

07

Combined Feeding

Complementary versus supplementary artificial feeding.

Complementary feeding

At each feed, the infant receives the breast first, then artificial milk completes the feed.

  • Give artificial feed only after both breasts are emptied.
  • Do not sweeten the artificial feed.
  • Bottle holes should not be large.
  • The source usually chooses humanized milk as nearest in composition to breast milk.

Supplementary feeding

Complete substitution of one or more breast feeds by artificial milk.

  • Working mother.
  • Twins.
  • Insufficient milk supply.
08

Cow’s Milk Protein Allergy

Types, clinical features, diagnosis and treatment.

Definition

An immune-mediated reaction to cow’s milk proteins, common in infants.

Minutes to 2 hours

IgE-mediated

Rapid onset.

Hours to days

Non-IgE-mediated

Delayed onset; examples include FPIAP and FPIES.

Both patterns

Mixed

Features of IgE and non-IgE disease.

Clinical features

  • Skin: urticaria, angioedema, eczema.
  • GI: vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain, blood/mucus in stool, reflux-like symptoms.
  • Respiratory: wheezing or rhinitis.
  • Severe IgE reaction may cause anaphylaxis.
  • Non-IgE disease may cause poor weight gain.

Diagnosis

  • Mainly clinical: history and response to elimination.
  • IgE-mediated: skin-prick test and/or specific IgE.
  • Oral food challenge is the diagnostic gold standard when needed.
  • Non-IgE: 2–4 week elimination followed by planned reintroduction.
Treatment
  1. Breastfed infant: maternal elimination of all dairy if indicated, with nutritional support.
  2. Formula-fed infant: extensively hydrolyzed formula first choice.
  3. Amino-acid formula for severe disease or failure of extensively hydrolyzed formula.
  4. Avoid unnecessary prolonged elimination and reassess periodically because most children eventually develop tolerance.
