02

Physiology of Milk Production

Preparation → initiation → maintenance, with hormonal control.

01

Preparation

  • Puberty: estrogen develops ducts; progesterone develops alveoli.
  • Pregnancy: estrogen, progesterone and placental lactogen develop the breast.
  • Prolactin is secreted, but placental hormones inhibit milk production.
02

Initiation

On delivery, the marked fall in sex hormones releases the pituitary from inhibition, allowing prolactin secretion and milk production.

03

Maintenance

  • Immediate, regular suckling.
  • Complete, regular breast evacuation.
  • Good maternal health and nutrition.
  • Lactagogues: Helba, phenothiazines and vitamin B.

Prolactin

  • Nipple suckling → vagal afferent → hypothalamus → anterior pituitary.
  • Milk secretion in acini.

Oxytocin

  • Nipple suckling → vagus → hypothalamus → posterior pituitary.
  • Myoepithelial contraction → milk ejection.

Modifiers

  • Growth hormone & thyroxine: anabolic effect.
  • PIF ↓ prolactin and milk secretion.
  • Fear, pain, anxiety and sadness inhibit ejection.
Which hormones control production and ejection?
Prolactin controls milk production; oxytocin controls milk ejection.
03

Stages & Composition of Milk

Colostrum, transitional milk, mature milk and comparison with fresh cow’s milk.

First 3–5 days

Colostrum

Thick yellow milk; SG 1.034; 2–10 ml/feed (40–60 ml/day); protein 2.2 g/dl, rich in Ig; low fat and sugar; 67 cal/dl; rich in colostrum corpuscles; ash 0.3 g/dl.

Promotes Bifidus flora, meconium passage and has high immunologic value.
Day 5 to 21

Transitional milk

Gradual increase in fat and carbohydrate with gradual decrease in protein.

After day 21

Mature milk

Thin white milk; SG 1.031; about 1000 ml/day; 70 cal/dl; protein 1 g%; sugar 7 g%; fat 4.2 g%; ash 0.2 g%; minimal colostrum corpuscles.

04

Advantages of Breast Feeding

Benefits to mother, infant and community.

Mother

  • Contraceptive method.
  • Prevents postpartum hemorrhage through better uterine involution.
  • Decreases breast cancer incidence.
  • Economic and needs no preparation.

Mother & infant

  • Creates a strong psychological relationship between mother and infant.

Community / infant

  • Sterile, cheap, readily available at any time.
  • Increased IQ and academic performance.

Nutrition & absorption

  • α-lactalbumin binds vitamin D, increasing effectiveness.
  • Iron, copper and zinc binding proteins improve absorption.
  • Glycosylated proteins resist proteolysis.
  • Oleic acid ↑ HDL-C and ↓ LDL-C/TG.

Bioactive support

  • Growth factors, enzymes and hormones are transported and modified to keep bioactivity.
  • Somatostatin, progesterone, TRH, carnitine and long-chain PUFAs.
  • Lipase and amylase compensate for immature pancreatic function.
  • Xanthine oxidase transports iron; glutathione peroxidase transports selenium.
Cow’s milk protein load

During the developing period, the higher protein load affects intellectual outcome, increases childhood diabetes frequency and may have long-term renal effects.

05

Anti-infective Properties

Direct antimicrobial, indirect, anti-inflammatory and immunomodulating actions.

Direct effect

  • Lactoferrin inhibits Staph. and E. coli.
  • B12/folate binding proteins deprive bacteria.
  • Bile salt-stimulated lipase kills Giardia and Entamoeba.
  • C3, C4, fibronectins and activated C3 are opsonic/chemotactic.
  • Lysozyme is acid-stable and bactericidal against gram-negative bacteria.
  • Mucins act against rotavirus; oligosaccharides block attachment.
  • Interferons induce an antiviral state.
  • Lactoperoxidase inhibits E. coli, Salmonella, Shigella, Pseudomonas and Vibrio.
  • Colostrum has 10⁶ leucocytes/ml: 90% neutrophils/macrophages; 10% lymphocytes.
  • IgM, IgG and especially secretory IgA; resistant to pH and proteolysis.
  • Triglyceride hydrolysis releases antiviral, antiprotozoal and antibacterial products.

Indirect effect

  • Bifidus growth factors promote L. bifidus.
  • Lactose → lactic + acetic acids → acidic intestinal content.
  • This reduces pathogen growth and increases Ca, P and Mg absorption.
  • Epidermal growth factors promote mucosal growth and maturation.

Anti-inflammatory

  • Reduces initiators and mediators.
  • Provides antioxidants, protease inhibitors, epithelial growth factors and mediator-degrading enzymes.
Immunomodulation

Long-term immune effects reduce risk of IDDM, lymphoma and Crohn’s disease. IL-1β enhances T-cell production; IL-6 augments antibody production; IL-8 attracts polymorphonuclear cells and CD8+ T-cells; IL-10 inhibits pro-inflammatory cytokines; IFN-α upregulates secretory component expression.

06

Adequacy of Breast Feeding

Infant and maternal signs, underfeeding, overfeeding and preterm milk.

Infant signs

  • Satisfied, quiet and sleeps for 2–3 hours.
  • Proper growth and weight gain.
  • Normal bowel habit 3–5/day and urine flow 6 times/day.
  • Test feed: weigh before and after feed.

Maternal signs

  • Let-down reflex.
  • Marked breast filling between feeds.

Underfeeding

  • Inadequate weight gain.
  • Irritable, hungry look.
  • Oliguria and constipation.

Overfeeding

  • Excessive weight gain.
  • Regurgitation and vomiting.
  • Polyuria, diarrhea and diaper rash.
Preterm milk compared with term milk

Higher calories, LCPUFA-rich lipids, nitrogen/protein, Na, Cl, K, iron, Mg, vitamins A and E; lower Ca and phosphorus. Platelet-activating factor acetylhydrolase and IL-10 protect against NEC. It matures to term-milk levels at about 4–6 weeks.

07

Management & Early Support

Antenatal education, peripartum practice and early follow-up.

01

Antenatal

  • Maternal nutrition.
  • Hunger cues, latch and positioning.
  • No supplementary milk despite small colostrum amounts.
  • Ability does not depend on breast size.
  • More suckling and emptying → more production.
  • Teach feeding frequency.
02

After delivery

  • Immediate skin-to-skin until first feed.
  • Delay routine procedures until first feed.
  • Vitamin K IM within 1–6 h, preferably after first feed: 1 mg for full-term/preterm; 0.5 mg if BW <1.5 kg.
  • Ensure 8–12 feeds/24 h.
03

Discharge & follow-up

  • Daily oral vitamin D 400 IU at discharge.
  • Review within 48–72 h.
  • Assess hydration, feeding, jaundice, stool, urine and condition.
  • Weight loss ≤7% and no further loss by day 5.
Hospital practices
  1. Document position, latch and milk transfer at least once each nursing shift.
  2. No water, glucose, formula or other fluids unless medically indicated.
  3. Avoid routine pacifiers postpartum.
  4. Empty the first breast before offering the second.
08

Technique of Nursing

Position, latch, milk transfer, frequency and post-feed assessment.

01

Position

  • No single correct position: cradle, cross-cradle, laid-back, clutch or side-lying.
  • Mother comfortable and relaxed.
  • Chest-to-chest; ear, shoulder and hips aligned.
  • Mouth level with nipple; pillows may prevent downward drift.
02

Latch

  • Bring baby to breast, not breast to baby.
  • Support at nape, not occiput.
  • C-hold behind areola; scissors hold may support small breasts.
  • Towel may support a large breast.
03

Milk transfer

  • Rhythmic suck-swallow-breathe with pauses.
  • Wide/deep jaw excursions.
  • Audible swallow with throat “ca” sound.
  • Adequate elimination for age.

Effective attachment

  • Lips flanged out; mouth wide open.
  • Chin close to or touching breast.
  • Tongue beneath breast and over alveolar ridge.

Ineffective latch

  • Tight pursed lips; lower lip pulled in.
  • Cheek dimpling and nipple pain.
  • Flattened nipple after feeding.
Frequency & post-feed

Feed on demand; wake if sleeping over 4 hours; minimum 8 feeds/24 h. Afterwards: nipple rounded and atraumatic, mother relaxed without musculoskeletal pain, infant calm and satiated, eructate twice, then place baby on the side rather than the back.

Underfeeding & Maternal Difficulties

Red flags, assessment and numbered clinical management blocks.

Underfeeding red flags

Apathy/weak cry or irritability, poor tone and turgor, concentrated scanty urine or crystals after 2 days, and no weight gain. Failure-to-thrive flags: continued weight loss beyond day 10, gain below the 10th percentile beyond 1 month, or a fall of 2 SD from a stable curve.

Test feed · before/after every feed × 3 daysTest weight · every 4 daysExpected increase · >100 g
Management of underfeeding
  1. Examine infant and mother for causes of reduced suckling.
  2. Confirm position, latch and transfer.
  3. Encourage rest, fluids and balanced protein-rich nutrition.
  4. First choice: mother’s expressed milk.
  5. Consider domperidone, plant galactagogues or oxytocin nasal spray.
  6. Formula may be given by dropper or syringe.
  7. Avoid bottles and pacifiers to prevent nipple confusion.
01

Delayed lactogenesis

Stress during labor, retained placenta or cesarean section. Treat with skin-to-skin and continued breastfeeding; refer if no improvement.

02

Low milk supply

Older age, obesity, type 1 DM, thyroid dysfunction. Confirm technique; feed ≥8/day for 10–15 min/breast; express after feeds; consider lactagogues/supplementation without bottles.

03

Nipple pain

Usually improper latch/position. Fissure with yellow crusting suggests bacterial infection: topical mupirocin. If candidiasis is suspected, treat mother and infant with antifungals.

04–05

Tongue-tie & nipple anomalies

Ankyloglossia may cause pain, poor latch and poor weight gain; frenotomy is surgical treatment. Proper latch may overcome flat/retracted nipples.

06

Engorgement

Infrequent feeding, sore nipples, disease or one-sided nursing. Grade 1: expression after suckling. Grade 2: before/after + antibiotic. Grade 3: antibiotics, stop suckling and drug therapy (stilbestrol).

07

Mastitis

Usually unilateral in 2–3%, after week 2. Warmth, erythema, tenderness, edema and systemic illness. Treat analgesics, oral antibiotics and emptying. Abscess needs surgery, IV antibiotics and temporary cessation from that breast.

Breastfeeding jaundice

  • Insufficient fluid intake in week 1.
  • Associated with dehydration and hypernatremia.

Breast milk jaundice

  • Persistently high indirect bilirubin in a thriving, well-fed infant.
  • Milk inhibits glucuronyl transferase; later onset, declines in weeks 2–3.
  • May need formula for 24–48 h plus phototherapy; resume breastfeeding after bilirubin falls.
10

Special Situations & Contraindications

Maternal infections and infant-related temporary or permanent conditions.

Open maternal TB

Stop breastfeeding for 2 weeks until cured; then feed while wearing a mask. Infant receives INH for 6 months, then tuberculin test: positive → no BCG; negative → give BCG.

Syphilitic mother

Can feed her infant who received penicillin for 2 weeks.

Temporary infant contraindications

  • Respiratory distress.
  • Complete cleft palate.
  • Prematurity <34 weeks due to suck-swallow incoordination.

Milk allergy

  • Now not considered a contraindication.
  • May cause diarrhea, vomiting, colic, occult blood, urticaria and wheezing.

Galactosemia

Only absolute contraindication nowadays. Galactose-1-uridyl transferase deficiency → galactose-1-phosphate accumulation. Reducing substances in urine; seizures, intellectual disability, cataract and hepatic involvement. Treat with soy milk or lactose-free formula.

Phenylketonuria

Phenylalanine hydroxylase deficiency → phenylalanine accumulation. Seizures, intellectual disability, fair skin/hair and blue eyes. Diagnose by metabolic screening; phenylalanine-restricted formula plus a little breastfeeding.

Lactose intolerance

Lactase deficiency causes lactose accumulation/fermentation. Abdominal distension, colic and watery diarrhea. Reducing substances in stool and low stool pH. Continue breastfeeding and add lactase enzyme.

What is the only absolute contraindication in the source?
Galactosemia.
Should breastfeeding stop in lactose intolerance?
No. Continue breastfeeding and add lactase enzyme.
