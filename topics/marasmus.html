01

Definition, Types & Incidence

The core definition and the two clinical types of marasmus.

Definition

Marasmus is a severe form of chronic undernutrition caused by insufficient caloric intake.

Nutritional marasmus

The primary form; it usually occurs between 6 months and 2 years.

Non-nutritional marasmus

The secondary form; it may occur at any age according to the underlying cause.

02

Etiology

Primary dietary causes and the major secondary systemic causes.

Nutritional marasmus: primary

Quantitative causes

  • Scanty milk supply in breast-fed or artificially fed infants.
  • Insufficient calories in the diet of older children.

Qualitative causes

  • Prolonged breast feeding without supplementation.
  • Diluted formula.
  • Excess fluids in the diet of older children.

Non-nutritional marasmus: secondary

GIT

Gastrointestinal causes

  • Malabsorption from inadequate digestion and absorption, as in cystic fibrosis.
  • Loss of food in prolonged or recurrent gastroenteritis.
  • Parasitic infestation, such as Giardia lamblia.
INFECTION

Chronic infections

Tuberculosis and bronchiectasis cause anorexia, food intolerance and increased body catabolism.

SYSTEMIC

Organ-system causes

  • Cardiovascular: congenital heart disease.
  • Hepatic: chronic liver disease or cirrhosis.
  • Respiratory: tuberculosis and cystic fibrosis.
  • Blood: chronic hemolytic anemia.
  • Renal: chronic renal failure.
METABOLIC & OTHER

Additional causes

  • Lactose intolerance, glycogen-storage disease and galactosemia.
  • Cleft palate, malignancy, starvation and anorexia nervosa.
PREMATURITY

Why premature infants are more susceptible

  • Low fat stores.
  • Weak suckling power.
  • Limited capacity for digestion and absorption.
03

Clinical Picture

Age, symptoms and the constant and associated signs of marasmus.

Typical age

Mostly the age of weaning; secondary marasmus may present at an older age.

Symptoms

Weight course

Failure to gain weight followed by progressive weight loss; symptoms of complications or of the underlying cause may also occur.

Hunger pattern

  • Irritability, crying and finger suckling with constipation; or
  • Starvation diarrhea: semisolid, dark-green, infrequent stool with mucus and debris.

Constant signs

01 · WEIGHT LOSS

Loss of weight

  • Growth curve below the expected weight for age: < 5th percentile.
  • First degree: 40% weight loss.
  • Second degree: 40-50% weight loss.
  • Third degree: > 50% weight loss.
02 · MUSCLE

Muscle wasting

Assessed by mid-upper arm circumference. Normal MUAC is > 15 cm at 1-5 years.

03 · SUBCUTANEOUS FAT

Loss of subcutaneous fat

  • Hypothermia, thin wrinkled skin and marked bony prominences.
  • First degree: loss of abdominal-wall subcutaneous fat.
  • Second degree: loss of buttock and thigh subcutaneous fat.
  • Third degree: loss of facial and buccal-pad fat, producing a senile or wise-man appearance.
  • Buccal fat is sacrificed last because it is unsaturated brown fat and is essential for good suckling.

Associated findings

Deficiencies

  • Vitamin deficiency as in kwashiorkor.
  • Mineral deficiency as in kwashiorkor.

Psychological & general

  • Anxious look, irritability, continuous crying and good appetite.
  • Weak pulse, markedly low blood pressure and markedly low temperature.
04

Prevention of PEM

Child-health, feeding and environmental measures that reduce protein-energy malnutrition.

FEEDING & GROWTH

Nutrition-focused prevention

  • Promote and encourage breast feeding.
  • Follow the growth pattern on growth curves; a flat curve is a red-flag sign.
  • Detect nutritional disorders early.
  • Provide nutritional education about the benefits of breast feeding and proper weaning.
INFECTION CONTROL

Disease prevention

  • Proper management of childhood infections, especially gastroenteritis.
  • Vaccination against measles, pertussis and tuberculosis.
GENERAL MEASURES

Community measures

  • Environmental sanitation.
  • Socioeconomic development.
  • Adequate food supply.
  • Prevention of infection.
05

Hospital Management of PEM

Admission criteria and immediate management of major complications.

Indications for hospitalization

  • Moderate or severe kwashiorkor.
  • Third-degree marasmus.
  • Marasmic kwashiorkor.
  • Any degree of PEM with complications.

Management of complications

  • Infection: appropriate antibiotics.
  • Shock: immediate IV lactated Ringer's solution, 20 ml/kg.
  • Correct dehydration and electrolyte disturbances properly.
  • Anemia: packed red blood cells.
  • Hypoglycemia: IV glucose.
  • Hypothermia: adequate clothing or a radiant warmer.
06

Nutritional Treatment

“Start low and go slow” through stabilization, transition and rehabilitation.

1-2 DAYS

Stabilization

Fluids to correct dehydration, with or without small feeds.

2-3 DAYS

Transition

80-100 cal/kg/day once the child becomes clinically stable.

THEN

Rehabilitation

Increase caloric intake or protein intake gradually.

Marasmus

AIM & AMOUNT

Increase calories gradually

  • Start with 80-100 cal/kg/day for 1-2 days.
  • Then increase to 150 cal/kg/day and continue upward according to tolerance.
TYPE & ROUTE

Age-appropriate feeding

  • Milk-fed: breast milk is best unless contraindicated.
  • Start with lactose-free formula, then standard formula.
  • With persistent diarrhea and stool pH < 6, use lactose-free formula longer.
  • Weaned child: a balanced high-calorie diet.
  • Route: oral or via nasogastric tube.

Kwashiorkor comparison

AIM & AMOUNT

Increase protein gradually

  • Start with 1 g/kg/day.
  • Increase gradually to 6 g/kg/day, then continue according to tolerance.
TYPE & ROUTE

Age-appropriate feeding

  • Milk-fed: breast milk is best unless contraindicated; start lactose-free then standard formula.
  • Weaned child: a protein-rich diet with egg, chicken, meat, fish, vegetables and fruits.
  • Route: oral; nasogastric in severe anorexia; parenteral in severe cases.
Vitamins & minerals

Vitamins A, B, C, D, E and folic acid may be given, with minerals such as magnesium, potassium and zinc. Iron is preferably delayed until the final stage because it may cause oxidant tissue damage and increase susceptibility to infection.

07

Therapeutic Feeds & Recovery

Special feeds, criteria of recovery and problems during treatment.

STABILIZATIONF-75Therapeutic milk with relatively low protein and sodium for the initial stabilization phase.
REHABILITATIONF-100Therapeutic milk with high protein and energy for rehabilitation.
READY TO USERUTFPeanut-based paste fortified with milk, oil, vitamins and minerals.

Criteria of recovery

  • In kwashiorkor, improvement proceeds through mood, appetite and edema regression; finally, muscle bulk improves and edema disappears.
  • Serum albumin normalizes; liver size may initially increase before regressing in kwashiorkor.
  • Initial weight loss is followed by weight gain.

Problems during treatment of kwashiorkor

Metabolic & volume problems

  • Hypokalemia with increased IV glucose.
  • Hypervolemia from over-transfusion of blood or plasma.
  • Diarrhea from transient carbohydrate, especially lactose, intolerance.
  • Nutritional recovery syndrome: a firm enlarged liver.
  • Excess protein load on the diseased liver may increase ammonia, leading to hepatic encephalopathy and liver-cell failure.

Refeeding syndrome

  • Severe hypophosphatemia after cellular phosphate uptake during the first week of refeeding.
  • May cause weakness, rhabdomyolysis, neutrophil dysfunction, cardiorespiratory failure, arrhythmias, seizures, altered consciousness or sudden death.
Why is iron delayed until the final treatment stage?
Because early iron may cause oxidant tissue damage and increase susceptibility to infection.
Which therapeutic milk is used during stabilization?
F-75, which has relatively low protein and sodium.
